# Win spellF1 with decoding, not deeper networks

**No: a neural network is not the clear-cut best method for this challenge, and the research points to a different place to spend your effort.** The one published network that solves exactly this task, a temporal convolutional network (TCN) that labels each frame dead/home/away, beat the best hand-written rules by about 7 points on possession and 33 points on dead-ball. But it learned from about 100K human-labelled windows taken from relatively clean data, and changing its architecture moved accuracy by only ±1.7 points ([Borghesi et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/8cc27103-7ee0-4243-8a04-0b256f77ad39/eswa_tcn_authors_accepted_version.pdf)). Nobody has benchmarked a network against gradient boosting on good window features for this task. On tabular-style features of this size, tree models remain the safe default ([Grinsztajn et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)). The larger, better-evidenced gains come from what happens *after* the per-frame scorer: constrained Viterbi decoding added about 8 points of event F1 in the closest IDSSE study ([PathCRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12080)), and segment-level refinement in action segmentation roughly doubles or triples segment F1 while frame accuracy barely moves ([MS-TCN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.01945); [ASRF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.06866)). spellF1 is a segment metric of the same kind. Your current plan is broadly right. The changes the research suggests are: make the decoder the centrepiece and tune it directly on spellF1, train mainly on *pattern-matched* degraded IDSSE, give the scorer Borghesi's "ball + 5 nearest players" input, split dead-ball detection from home/away, add touch-gating and restart rules as decoder constraints, and keep the NN as a time-boxed second scorer that must earn its place. Throughout, claims with a link are cited evidence; sentences marked *(our inference)* are the writers' reasoning and have not been tested.

## The published numbers say "temporal model", not "neural network"

The closest prior work is Borghesi, Costa, Morra and Lamberti (2023). They label every frame dead/home/away from tracking data with labels from a human "three-button timer" operator, which is essentially your task. They trained on 100K sliding windows from 4 matches and tested on 2 unseen matches. They used only the 8 of 35 matches with the *least* missing ball, so their numbers are on cleaner data than yours ([Borghesi et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/8cc27103-7ee0-4243-8a04-0b256f77ad39/eswa_tcn_authors_accepted_version.pdf)). On the same data, the rule-based methods scored **64.5% (Link & Hoernig), 75.4% (Khaustov & Mozgovoy) and 79.1% (Morra et al.)** team-possession accuracy, against **86.2%** for the TCN. A ball-only decision tree managed **56.0%** on dead-vs-alive, against **89.2%** for the TCN (same source). The TCN reads a 64-frame window with the target at frame 48, so it deliberately looks into the future. The authors argue this matters because the signs of a foul, such as players stopping or walking toward the referee, appear only after it happens.

Three details in that paper matter more than the headline. First, every architecture variant landed within **82.93% ± 1.71%** global accuracy, so the input and the training data mattered more than the network design. Second, the ablations show that **ball + the 5 nearest players gave 84.98% possession accuracy, the same as using all players (84.95%)**. With x,y only and no team flags, accuracy fell to 51.89%, which is chance, so team identity is essential. Third, dead-vs-alive leaned heavily on velocities and an "outside the pitch" flag. Removing them dropped dead/alive accuracy to about 75% ([Borghesi et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/8cc27103-7ee0-4243-8a04-0b256f77ad39/eswa_tcn_authors_accepted_version.pdf)). Everything that worked is a feature you can hand-build for LightGBM. That makes "NN beats rules" an established result, while "NN beats a gradient-boosted model on good window features" is untested.

Related evidence points the same way. Lang et al. predicted ball-in-play status from **player tracking alone with up to 92% frame accuracy on unseen matches**, using classical learners where AdaBoost was best, not a deep network ([Lang et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/4899fef7e03b4b3aaf5e0d0f1ccb0e3a)). The strongest ball-free result is **PathCRF: 92.4% frame-level team-possession accuracy on an IDSSE test match with no ball input at all** ([PathCRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12080)). Its own ablation, though, credits much of its gain to the decoder (next section), and it models individual players as graph nodes, so it relies on persistent player IDs. Your data has none. Kim et al.'s Bi-LSTM recovered team possession at **85.3% with the ball fully hidden**, and at **96.7% with 80% of ball frames hidden** ([Kim et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08206)). That second figure suggests your 59% visible ball is a lot of signal. Their masking was random, however, while yours is probably clustered into long gaps during aerial balls and camera cuts, which is harder *(our inference)*.

The data-size argument settles the question for this project. You have 5 minutes of ground truth, about 7,500 frames or 17 possessions. That is enough to sanity-check a model but far too little to train or select one. The only route to Borghesi-scale supervision is IDSSE: 7 DFL matches, about 1.0M frames at 25 Hz with per-frame BallPossession and BallStatus, CC-BY ([Bassek et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11787359/)). Any learned model, tree or network, will therefore be trained on someone else's clean in-stadium data and run on your noisy broadcast data. The bottleneck is that domain shift, not model capacity *(our inference)*. A more flexible network is also better at memorising quirks of the training domain, so it is the riskier choice under shift.

## spellF1 punishes flicker, so the decoder is where points live

spellF1 matches whole H/A runs by temporal IoU, which is the same family as the F1@k metric in temporal action segmentation, where predictions and ground-truth segments are matched one-to-one ([Ding et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.10352v5)). That literature shows a pattern your team should take seriously. In MS-TCN, going from 1 to 4 refinement stages raised **F1@50 from 21.5 to 64.5 while frame accuracy stayed between 78% and 81%** ([MS-TCN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.01945)). Adding boundary-regression refinement (ASRF) gained another **10 to 15 points of F1@50** across three datasets, again with only 1 to 4 points of frame accuracy. In ASRF's head-to-head on one backbone, simple Gaussian smoothing scored worse than relabelling short segments, which in turn scored worse than boundary-based refinement ([ASRF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.06866)). A model with good per-frame accuracy can still score badly on segments if it flickers.

The arithmetic for your metric is stark *(our inference)*. A spurious 0.5 s "A" blip inside a 10 s home possession adds one false positive *and* cuts the true H run into two pieces, each of which can fall below IoU 0.5 against the truth. At the upper thresholds, one flicker turns one true positive into zero true positives and two false positives. A boundary placed 1 s late on a 10 s spell, by contrast, still gives IoU of about 0.82 (9 s overlap over 11 s union), which passes every threshold up to 0.7. So **getting the number of segments right matters far more than placing boundaries to the frame**, and the decoder should penalise short runs much more heavily than per-frame log-loss suggests. Dead-ball runs are not scored as spells, but they matter twice: they cut H/A runs, and labelling a short dead gap as H/A merges two true spells into one predicted spell, which costs two misses and one false positive.

All the useful structure can be written as hard masks or soft costs inside one exact dynamic program. A plain HMM's self-loop implies a geometric duration whose most likely length is one frame. A semi-Markov model, or an "expanded-state" HMM with a chain of k_min states per label, enforces a minimum duration and a realistic length distribution ([Murphy](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~murphyk/Papers/segment.pdf)). Speaker-diarization systems enforce minimum turn length the same way and tune their switch probability per dataset ([Diez et al. 2019](https://fit.vut.cz/research/group/speech/public/publi/2019/diez_is2019_192813.pdf)). PathCRF supplies the football-specific proof that forbidding illegal transitions in Viterbi is worth about 8 points ([PathCRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12080)).

Four football-specific constraints follow from this. None has been evaluated in published work, so all four are *our inference*. First, **touch-gating**: allow a direct H↔A switch only near a detected touch candidate, such as a ball-acceleration spike above about 4 m/s², the threshold Link & Hoernig used ([Borghesi's description](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/8cc27103-7ee0-4243-8a04-0b256f77ad39/eswa_tcn_authors_accepted_version.pdf)), or an IMM "kick" mode. Make it a soft penalty rather than a ban, because 41% of the ball is missing and touches inside gaps will be invisible. Second, **dead-ball structure**: impose a minimum duration of about 2 s on D, and let restart evidence decide the label after D. Vidal-Codina's set-piece templates need only player positions and identify the restarting player for kickoffs, throw-ins, corners and goal kicks at 92–100% recall, though free kicks are worse at about 89% recall and 82% precision ([Vidal-Codina et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.00804v2)). Third, **anchors**: clamp frames where the ball is visible within about 1–1.5 m of only one team's players, which splits the decode into independent, drift-free pieces. Fourth, **back-dating**: after decoding, move each switch to the earliest controlled touch by the new team within about −2 s to +0.5 s, in the spirit of ASRF's boundary refinement. Finally, keep the decoded labels for the frame-level tie-break. Reverting to argmax would bring the flicker back.

Tune the decoder on the metric itself. Grid-search a handful of knobs (switch penalty β, emission temperature α, H/A minimum duration) under leave-one-match-out on degraded IDSSE, scoring spellF1 directly *(our inference)*. The temperature matters because adjacent 25 fps frames are almost identical. Treating them as independent evidence makes the emission term roughly 25× overconfident. Diarization systems handle this with a posterior scale factor and downsampling by 25 ([DIHARD III](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01477)). Expect the best β to over-penalise switching compared with the "probabilistic" value. Fit the duration priors from IDSSE *team spells* (gamma-shaped, as for J-League possession times; [arXiv 2308.05460](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.05460)). Do not fit them from touch-level "control phases", which average about 1.4 s and are a different definition ([Springer chapter](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-319-24560-7_13.pdf)). Write the decoder yourselves in numba. edhsmm is archived and its author does not guarantee correctness, and pyhsmm uses sampling, so it is not deterministic ([edhsmm](https://github.com/poypoyan/edhsmm)). A 3-state expanded-chain Viterbi over 200k frames runs in well under a second *(our inference)*.

## Training data fidelity beats model capacity

Your plan trains on "confident labels + degraded IDSSE". The research supports this but changes the weighting. Confident rule-labelled frames are a biased sample: close control and obvious dead balls. A model trained mainly on them will be sure of itself on easy frames and arbitrary on the hard 41%. Self-training works only when the student both corrects the teacher's mistakes and extends to inputs the teacher abstained on, and both effects need the student not to simply re-learn the teacher's features ([Lang, Sontag & Vijayaraghavan 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16043)). Naive pseudo-labelling overfits its own errors, and a guaranteed floor of trusted labels in every mini-batch is one fix ([Arazo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02983v5)). Degraded IDSSE is that trusted floor, because it has true labels on hard frames too. **So make IDSSE the main training set and the confident target frames a down-weighted supplement**, and run at most one round of self-training *(our inference)*.

Degradation must copy your data's *pattern* of noise, not just its rate. Random input dropout is not the same as augmentation that targets realistic missingness ([DropIn study](https://arpi.unipi.it/bitstream/11568/1000157/2/dropinjournal.pdf)). Measure the target match's ball-gap length distribution and where gaps occur, then resample gaps of those lengths into IDSSE in similar contexts. Also add position jitter, strip player IDs, and reduce IDSSE to about 20 visible players. Then run a cheap check *(our inference)*: train a quick classifier to tell degraded-IDSSE windows from target windows. An AUC near 0.5 means the degradation matches. A high AUC, together with the features that drive it, tells you what to fix. This check does the useful part of domain-adversarial training without its model-selection problem ([AdaTime](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08321)).

One risk is easy to miss. IDSSE's possession field is "defined by the player in control of the ball", and the paper does not say how contested balls, aerial passes or dead-ball frames are coded, or whether the labels are manual ([Bassek et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11787359/)). If the organisers' 5-minute ground truth uses a different convention, for example keeping the ball with a team through loose-ball spells as a three-button operator would, a model trained on IDSSE will be systematically "wrong" in a way no feature can fix. **Spend an hour on day 1 comparing IDSSE label behaviour around passes and duels with your 17 ground-truth possessions** *(our inference)*.

SkillCorner's open data adds a resource the plan does not use: 10 A-League matches of *broadcast* tracking at 10 fps, with a per-frame team-possession label and an `is_detected` flag marking extrapolated off-camera players ([SkillCorner Open Data](https://skillcorner.github.io/opendata/)). It has no dead-ball flag, and its possession method is undocumented, so it cannot replace IDSSE. It can calibrate your degradation, since it shows what real broadcast detection gaps look like, and serve as an out-of-domain H/A validation set *(our inference; not verified hands-on)*.

On the input side, give the scorer Borghesi's winning recipe in tabular form *(our inference from [Borghesi](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/8cc27103-7ee0-4243-8a04-0b256f77ad39/eswa_tcn_authors_accepted_version.pdf))*. That means the ball (or filtered ball) with missing flags and gap length, plus the 5 nearest players *per team* sorted by distance to the ball, with velocities. Sorting by distance also solves the no-ID ordering problem. Compute these over multi-scale acausal windows (about 0.2 s, 1 s and 3 s, with both past and future offsets). For the dead-ball branch, add velocity-collapse, out-of-pitch and set-piece-template features from all players, since dead/alive benefits from the full team. Split the model in two, dead-vs-alive and then home-vs-away given alive, matching Borghesi's best two-branch design. Treat "ball missing" as a feature, not a dead-ball rule, because on broadcast data missing often means off-camera rather than out of play.

Downgrade the IMM filter from centrepiece to helper. No football paper reports IMM ball-tracking accuracy. Multiple-model filters do beat single-model ones for manoeuvring targets in robotics ([Kwok & Fox](https://core.ac.uk/download/9031182.pdf)), and Link & Hoernig used a simple RTS smoother on ball segments ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0179953)). PathCRF found that reconstructing the ball and then applying rules is much weaker than inferring possession directly ([PathCRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12080)). The IMM's real value is bridging short gaps and flagging kicks as touch candidates for the decoder. Do not spend days on long-gap ball imputation *(our inference)*.

## Recommended design: where the neural network fits

The recommended pipeline keeps your skeleton. The emphasis moves to the decoder and the training data, and the NN becomes a challenger, not a pillar.

| Stage | What to build | Evidence vs inference |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clean + ball helper | Clean ATD; RTS/IMM smoother bridging gaps under ~1 s; ball-acceleration spikes and IMM kick mode as **touch candidates** | Touch threshold cited; IMM benefit is inference |
| 2. Anchors | Ball visible and within ~1–1.5 m of one team only; set-piece templates giving D, then restarting team | Radii and templates cited ([Vidal-Codina](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.00804v2)); use as anchors is inference |
| 3. Features | Ball + missing flag + gap length; 5 nearest per team by distance to ball, with velocities; team centroid, length, width, speed; multi-scale acausal windows | Input set cited (Borghesi ablation); window choice inference |
| 4. Scorer A (core) | Two-stage LightGBM (D vs alive; H vs A given alive), trained on pattern-matched degraded IDSSE plus down-weighted confident target frames, `deterministic=True, force_row_wise=True`, fixed seed and threads | Determinism cited ([docs](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Parameters.html)); weighting inference |
| 5. Scorer B (optional NN) | Small permutation-invariant TCN or Bi-GRU on the same ball + 5-nearest-per-team input with missing flags. **One student, two days.** If it wins, average its probabilities with LightGBM before decoding | Architecture cited; ensembling inference |
| 6. Decoder | Numba expanded-state Viterbi: min durations, IDSSE-fitted duration priors, emission temperature α, switch penalty β, soft touch-gating on H↔A, D→H/A tied to restart evidence, clamped anchors | Components cited separately; combination unevaluated |
| 7. Refine + output | Back-date each switch to the earliest new-team touch in [−2 s, +0.5 s]; keep decoded labels for the tie-break; write CSV; log a hash | Inference (ASRF-style) |
| 8. Selection | Tune β, α, d_min and choose A vs A+B by **spellF1 under leave-one-IDSSE-match-out**; use the 5-minute GT only as a final sanity check | Inference; with 17 possessions, differences under ~1 boundary error are noise |

Is the NN worth it? Only as Scorer B, under a hard rule set before you start *(our inference)*: keep it only if it beats LightGBM on leave-one-IDSSE-match-out spellF1, averaged over 3 seeds, *and* does not hurt the 17-possession check. Borghesi's ±1.7% architecture spread suggests the honest expectation is a small gain at best. PyTorch reproducibility is not guaranteed across platforms or versions ([PyTorch notes](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/notes/randomness.html)), so if you ship it, generate the final labels on one pinned machine with `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)`, fixed threads and `num_workers=0`. Do not try PathCRF itself. Its player-edge formulation needs identities you do not have. What carries over is its lesson that constrained decoding, not the backbone, delivers the big jump.

## Conclusion

The question "is a neural network best?" turns out to be the wrong one for this metric. The literature shows that dead/home/away labelling needs *temporal, acausal context* and *team-aware inputs near the ball*, and that segment metrics reward *consistent segments* far more than better per-frame scores. Those are properties of the features and the decoder, and a tree model with a well-tuned Viterbi pass has all of them. An NN is a legitimate challenger, not a requirement. The biggest unknowns are untested in any published work, and they are where your own experiments create value. Does pattern-matched degradation make IDSSE transfer to broadcast data? Does IDSSE's possession convention match the organisers'? How much do touch-gating and restart rules add on top of plain Viterbi? Reporting a clean ablation of those three points would itself be a contribution, whatever the leaderboard says.
